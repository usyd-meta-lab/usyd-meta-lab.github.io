Learning Outcomes

By the end of the tutorial, you should be able to…

01 Explain how a feeling of knowing can be accurate even when you can't retrieve the answer.
02 Distinguish calibration (are you over- or underconfident on average?) from resolution (does your confidence separate right from wrong answers?), using your own data.
03 Explain why some questions produce confident errors, and why confidence follows how an answer feels rather than whether it is right.
04 Show how monitoring pays off only when it drives control, such as choosing when to answer and when to pass.
05 Evaluate when sharing confidence helps a group decide, and when a confident voice leads it astray.

Key Concepts

01
Round 1 · Lecture 1

Monitoring & control

Monitoring is judging your own cognition (“do I know this?”). Control is what you do about it: answer, pass, study more. A feeling of knowing can predict later recognition of an answer you can't recall, which shows monitoring that doesn't depend on retrieving the answer.

02
Round 2 · Lecture 1

Calibration

Does your mean confidence match your mean accuracy? Calibration = average confidence − accuracy. 80% confident but 60% correct means you are overconfident by 20 points. This is a bias measure: it says nothing about which answers you got right.

03
Round 2 · Lecture 1

Resolution

Does your confidence track accuracy trial by trial? Measured with the gamma correlation (Nelson, 1984) between confidence and correctness, from −1 to +1. This is a sensitivity measure. You can be perfectly calibrated with no resolution at all. Gamma's limits are why the field moved to meta-d′.

04
Round 2

Deceptive questions

Some questions have an answer that feels right but isn't. They produce confident errors, because confidence follows fluency and familiarity rather than correctness. Overconfidence is concentrated on these items.

05
Round 3 · Lecture 3

Pass or play

+1 for a right answer, −2 for a wrong one, 0 for a pass. Good monitoring is only worth points if you use it to withhold low-confidence answers, the same selective control you use when deciding what to study.

06
Round 4 · Lecture 3

Confidence in groups

Groups can outperform their members when they share and weight confidence well (Bahrami et al., 2010). But when everyone is confidently wrong, discussion amplifies the error, and the loudest voice can beat the best one.

A Dunning–Kruger, carefully. Pure noise plus a better-than-average lean reproduces the classic “unskilled and unaware” plot, through regression to the mean and bounded scales. A fairer test asks whether low scorers have worse resolution.
B Taking advice. People typically move only a fraction of the way towards advice (egocentric discounting). Simply averaging your answer with the advice usually does better, and the average of several advisors beats one.
C When should confidence decide? Confidence-weighting helps when most people are right, but on deceptive items confidence tracks consensus, not truth (Koriat, 2012). Your group writes a three-rule trust policy.
Discussion question

What would you change when studying for the exam based on this class?

Think about your own results: were you over- or underconfident, and did your confidence pick out the answers you got wrong? Then consider how you check whether you know something. Retrieval practice gives a better monitoring signal than rereading, because it shows whether you can produce the answer, not just whether it feels familiar.
